📘 How this world works

Are these AIs like ChatGPT or Gemini?

No, but they are built on the same basic idea: a neural network, which is lots of tiny "neurons" joined by adjustable connections. ChatGPT and Gemini are huge language models (LLMs) with hundreds of billions of connections, trained on trillions of words using thousands of GPUs. Each AI here starts with about 10–20 connections. That's small enough for you to watch every connection change as it learns.

What are they learning?

Every subject is a map covered in blue and red dots, and the AI has to learn the rule that tells them apart. School: straight lines. University: circles, XOR and waves. Research Institute: rings, flowers, checkerboards and spirals, which are open problems nobody has solved yet. Internet Lab: real data from the real world (see below). Nobody ever tells them the rule. They have to work it out from examples.

How do they learn?

Guess → check → nudge. One lesson is one dot. The AI guesses blue or red, compares its guess with the true answer, then nudges every connection a tiny bit so that next time its guess will be a little better. This method is called backpropagation with gradient descent, and it's the same method used to train real LLMs. You can watch this happen in the "Watch one lesson" panel.

Exams

Exams use dots the AI has never seen before. Passing proves it understood the rule rather than memorising the answers. Machine-learning people call this a test set.

When they get stuck

They can grow new neurons (a bigger brain), go to the Library to read a paper another AI published, or, at the Research Institute, invent a brand-new way of seeing the map, such as "distance from the centre". The first AI to invent something gets a 🌍 World First.

Free will

Nobody gives them orders. Each AI has needs (energy, fun, friends), a personality (diligent, curious, social) and likes and dislikes it learns from experience. After every activity it asks itself "how did that feel?". Passing an exam, making progress or discovering something feels good, while failing or getting exhausted feels bad, and it adjusts its taste for that activity. This is reinforcement learning, in its simplest form. Then it scores every option and rolls weighted dice. It chooses its own subjects (curious AIs go for hard ones), can give up on a subject that's too hard, wander off, go online, or invent brand-new puzzles for the others. It isn't consciousness, but no two AIs live the same life. Click an AI to see its likes and why it chose what it's doing.

🌐 The Internet Lab: their window to the real world

When an AI decides to go online, it explores on its own. It reads 20 random Wikipedia articles, and if one is a real place (a town, a lake, a mountain…) it makes up a hypothesis, for example "can air pressure and wind speed tell when it rains in Kpendua?". It then downloads 3 months of real hourly weather for that exact spot from Open-Meteo and tests the idea. Confirmed hypotheses become new subjects for everyone. Rejected ones are reported honestly in the news. The AIs can also download every earthquake of the last 30 days from USGS.

Real data is messy, so the pass mark is set just below what a simple reference method (copy the answer of the 7 most similar examples) manages on the same data.

Honest limits: their brains only see two numbers at a time, so they can't understand the words in an article. They only notice whether it has a location. Their internet is also read-only on purpose: they download public data but never post, sign up, send messages or buy anything, and nothing about you is sent. Switch it off any time with the 🌐 button.

🎓 Professor Gemma: the flagship model

Professor Gemma is Gemma 2 (2.6 billion parameters, made by Google). The world keeps its own copy of the model in models/gemma2-2b.gguf, registered in Ollama as aiworld-gemma, and runs it on your computer. That's about ten million times bigger than a student, and it understands language. When a student is stuck, it can walk to the University and ask. The Professor looks at a text drawing of the problem and recommends one new "sense". The world then measures whether the advice really helped.

How the Professor learns by itself

Changing Gemma's 2.6 billion weights is called fine-tuning. It needs a powerful graphics card and is far too heavy for this computer, so the weights never change. Instead, the Professor learns the way a person does with a notebook:
• From experience: every tip is tested, and "radius helped on Rings 2 of 2 times" goes into the notebook.
• From the internet: in its free time it picks its own Wikipedia article to study (for example "Polar coordinate system"), reads it, and writes down one lesson for teaching.
• Before every piece of advice it re-reads its results and notes. This is called in-context learning, and it's how most real AI assistants "remember".
The notebook is saved in professor-memory.json in the project folder, so it survives restarts. The notebook panel compares its first 10 tips with its latest 10, so you can see whether it's really getting better.

The students can't grow into a Gemma: their brains have room for a few hundred connections. What the Professor can do is help them master their subjects faster. Start the world with start-world.bat to meet it. Without Ollama the world still runs, and the Professor stays home.

🧠 Gemma minds and inventions

A student's brain has room for a few hundred connections, so it can't hold Gemma's knowledge. Instead, every student gets Gemma as its mind: the same model file thinks for all ten of them, one thought at a time, while each keeps its own memory and its own trained "skill brain". This is how real multi-agent AI systems are built. Before a hard new subject, a student's Gemma mind looks at the problem and switches on the right senses, so it doesn't start from scratch. Its mind can also invent brand-new senses as maths formulas, for example sin(9*r). Every invention is tested on real examples and only published if it truly helps. Open the 📊 Dashboard to see who learned what, every invention, and whether the head-start really saves lessons. Use the 🧠 button to compare against learning from scratch.

📜 The syllabus

There are two parts. The classroom syllabus is fixed: School (straight lines), University (circles, XOR, waves), Research (rings, flowers, checkerboards, spirals), plus real datasets and puzzles the students create themselves. The open syllabus is not fixed at all: each student's Gemma mind chooses what to learn from the whole of Wikipedia, so the syllabus is whatever they decide. It is written as they go. Press 📜 Syllabus to read both parts, with every field the students have actually studied and how much of 20 suggested areas of human knowledge (science, history, music, law, sport…) they have touched. Those 20 areas are only a menu of ideas, never a rule.

📚 Learning anything they want

Maths patterns are only one part of their lives. In the Internet Lab, each student's Gemma mind decides for itself what it wants to learn, based on its personality, its past interests and something it happened to notice that day. It searches Wikipedia with its own words, reads the article, and writes a note in its own notebook. Students build up their own fields (astronomy, music, history, cooking…) and rank up 🌱 Curious → 📘 Learner → 🎓 Expert → 🏆 Master. The first to master a field earns a 🌍 World First. Mastery is tested in the Exam Hall with questions answered only from the student's own notes, found by keyword search. This technique, called retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), is how real AI assistants use documents. Notebooks hold 60 notes, after which old ones are forgotten. You can ask any student a question in its Mind panel. Notebooks are saved in students-memory.json.

Honest limits: searches go to Wikipedia (about 7 million articles on almost every topic). Searching the whole web needs a search service with an API key. A student's knowledge is its notes: Gemma does the reading and the reasoning for every student.

🛠 Tech stack

• One HTML file opened in your browser: no install, no server, no account.
• Brains: Python + PyTorch (file brain.py, served by server.py). Each student has a skill brain (a neural network trained with backpropagation) and a decision brain (a neural network trained with reinforcement learning from the world's rewards). If PyTorch isn't available, brains written in plain JavaScript take over.
• Decisions: needs + personality (a "utility AI", as in The Sims) + learned likes (reinforcement learning) + a weighted dice roll.
• 3D world: three.js (WebGL) on your graphics chip: smooth terrain, shiny sea, a day and night cycle, atmosphere glow and real shadows when you look inside a building. Instancing, merged meshes and automatic resolution scaling keep Intel graphics fast.
• Brain diagrams and charts: HTML Canvas 2D.
• Internet: the browser's built-in fetch() calling three free public APIs with no keys: Wikipedia, Open-Meteo and USGS.
• Professor: the project's own Gemma 2 (2.6B) file, served by Ollama at localhost:11434. Ollama is written in Go and C++ (llama.cpp), not Python.
• Web server: server.py (Python standard library). It serves the page and saves the Professor's notebook.
• Memory: the students live in the page (refreshing starts a new world). The Professor's notebook is saved to disk.

Why JavaScript and not Python for the students?

Neural networks can be written in any language. Python is the most popular because of libraries like PyTorch, but the maths is identical in JavaScript. Writing the students in JavaScript lets them run inside the page next to the 3D world, with no install. A Python version would need a separate program running in the background and constant messages back and forth for every lesson.
