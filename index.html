📘 How this world works

Are these AIs like ChatGPT or Gemini?

No, but they are built on the same basic idea: a neural network, which is lots of tiny "neurons" joined by adjustable connections. ChatGPT and Gemini are huge language models (LLMs) with hundreds of billions of connections, trained on trillions of words using thousands of GPUs. Each AI here starts with about 10–20 connections. That's small enough for you to watch every connection change as it learns.

What are they learning?

Every subject is a map covered in blue and red dots, and the AI has to learn the rule that tells them apart. School: straight lines. University: circles, XOR and waves. Research Institute: rings, flowers, checkerboards and spirals, which are open problems nobody has solved yet. Internet Lab: real data from the real world (see below). Nobody ever tells them the rule. They have to work it out from examples.

How do they learn?

Guess → check → nudge. One lesson is one dot. The AI guesses blue or red, compares its guess with the true answer, then nudges every connection a tiny bit so that next time its guess will be a little better. This method is called backpropagation with gradient descent, and it's the same method used to train real LLMs. You can watch this happen in the "Watch one lesson" panel.

Exams

Exams use dots the AI has never seen before. Passing proves it understood the rule rather than memorising the answers. Machine-learning people call this a test set.

When they get stuck

They can grow new neurons (a bigger brain), go to the Library to read a paper another AI published, or, at the Research Institute, invent a brand-new way of seeing the map, such as "distance from the centre". The first AI to invent something gets a 🌍 World First.

Free will

Nobody gives them orders. Each AI has needs (energy, fun, friends), a personality (diligent, curious, social) and likes and dislikes it learns from experience. After every activity it asks itself "how did that feel?". Passing an exam, making progress or discovering something feels good, while failing or getting exhausted feels bad, and it adjusts its taste for that activity. This is reinforcement learning, in its simplest form. Then it scores every option and rolls weighted dice. It chooses its own subjects (curious AIs go for hard ones), can give up on a subject that's too hard, wander off, go online, or invent brand-new puzzles for the others. It isn't consciousness, but no two AIs live the same life. Click an AI to see its likes and why it chose what it's doing.

🌐 The Internet Lab: their window to the real world

When an AI decides to go online, it explores on its own. It picks a city, asks Wikipedia for real places nearby (a campus, a park, a museum…) and makes up a hypothesis about one, for example "can air pressure and wind speed tell when it rains at this museum?". It then downloads 3 months of real hourly weather for that exact spot from Open-Meteo and tests the idea. Confirmed hypotheses become new subjects for everyone. Rejected ones are reported honestly in the news. The AIs can also download every earthquake of the last 30 days from USGS.

Real data is messy, so the pass mark is set just below what a simple reference method (copy the answer of the 7 most similar examples) manages on the same data.

Honest limits: their brains only see two numbers at a time, so they can't read or understand text. They only use Wikipedia to find a place's location. Their internet is also read-only on purpose: they download public data but never post, sign up, send messages or buy anything, and nothing about you is sent. Switch it off any time with the 🌐 button.

📜 The syllabus: seven levels

Students move up like pupils through school. Level 1 Foundations (straight lines) → 2 Shapes → 3 Curves & Logic (ellipse, XOR, waves) → 4 Patterns (rings, checkerboards, plus a real-world dataset) → 5 Mastery (flower, spiral) → 6 Invention (create a new puzzle or discover a new sense) → 7 Open research. To be promoted, a student must pass every subject of its level and meet that level's extra requirement. Then it moves on to the next level, and so on. The order follows curriculum learning (easy before hard), the dataset order of TensorFlow Playground, UNESCO's education levels and Bloom's taxonomy. Press 📜 Syllabus to read the levels and see who is where.

🛠 Tech stack

• One HTML file opened in your browser: no install, no server, no account.
• Brains: Python + PyTorch (file brain.py, served by server.py). Each student has a skill brain (a neural network trained with backpropagation) and a decision brain (a neural network trained with reinforcement learning from the world's rewards). The page has no brain of its own: it needs the Python server (start-world.bat).
• Decisions: needs + personality (a "utility AI", as in The Sims) + learned likes (reinforcement learning) + a weighted dice roll.
• 3D world: three.js (WebGL) on your graphics chip: smooth terrain, shiny sea, a day and night cycle, atmosphere glow and real shadows when you look inside a building. Instancing, merged meshes and automatic resolution scaling keep Intel graphics fast.
• Brain diagrams and charts: HTML Canvas 2D.
• Internet: the browser's built-in fetch() calling three free public APIs with no keys: Wikipedia, Open-Meteo and USGS.
• Web server: server.py (Python standard library). It serves the page and hosts the PyTorch brains.
• Memory: the students' networks live in the Python server. Reloading the page starts a new world.
